\section{The long run}\label{sec:sp:longrun}

The exhaustibility of discoveries gives the model a hard long-run question. Cumulative virgin extraction is bounded by~\eqref{eq:sp:sum:cumulative-N}, so the economy cannot draw on nature forever; what it can do is stretch a finite material budget through the recycling loop, hold material in its two anthropogenic stocks, the capital stock and the waste stock, and, if technology permits, keep a closed loop turning forever. With the minimum material requirement read as a hard floor, the question sharpens from ``what does the economy converge to'' to ``does the economy survive'': there is no dematerialized mode to retire into, and holding the floor forever is the only alternative to collapse. This section asks which long-run states are feasible and which the planner ends up in. Two primitives organise the answer, whether production has a material floor ($\bar R=0$ or $\bar R>0$) and whether the yield ceiling is hard or soft. Their four combinations are the \emph{cells} of the classification, and the goal of the section is to attach to each cell the long-run state the economy reaches. Technology trends throughout; that is the maintained regime of Section~\ref{sec:sp:longrun:scope}, and the stationary alternative is a benchmark outside the classification, carried in Appendix~\ref{sec:app:stat}. We proceed through the maintained assumptions (Section~\ref{sec:sp:longrun:scope}), a block of accounting facts that hold on every feasible path (Section~\ref{sec:sp:longrun:budget}), the definition of the three long-run states (Section~\ref{sec:sp:longrun:class}), the taxonomy that assigns a state to each cell (Section~\ref{sec:sp:longrun:taxonomy}) and a sketch of each assignment (Section~\ref{sec:sp:longrun:sketches}). Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs} carries the arguments, separating what holds for general primitives from what is constructed for the workhorse forms of Appendix~\ref{sec:app:workhorse}, and Appendix~\ref{sec:app:suff} records what sufficiency adds.

\subsection{Scope: maintained long-run assumptions}\label{sec:sp:longrun:scope}

The system of Section~\ref{sec:sp:opt} carries six states, is non-autonomous through the technology trends, is non-convex through the decay function ($\theta'<0$) and the material floor, and switches between corner and interior regimes on several margins. We restrict the scope of the long-run analysis with four assumptions, and we are explicit about what they rule out and what they do not.

Throughout this section, growth and decay rates are per-period log rates: a variable $Z$ ``grows at rate $g$'' when $\ln Z_{t+1}-\ln Z_t\to g$, and ``decays at rate $\nu$'' when it grows at rate $-\nu$, so that products of trending variables simply add their rates.

\begin{assumption}[trending technology]\label{ass:lr:tech}
Final-goods technology combines TFP growth and material-augmenting progress at constant rates,
\begin{align}
    F\left(K^Y,R,P,t\right) &= A_t\,\tilde{\mathcal F}\left(K^Y,B_tR^{e},P\right)\ \text{for }R\ge\bar R,\quad 0\text{ otherwise}, \label{eq:sp:lr:trendF} \\
    \text{and }\frac{A_{t+1}}{A_t}&=e^{g_A},\ g_A\ge0, \qquad
    \frac{B_{t+1}}{B_t}=e^{g_B},\ g_B\ge0, \notag
\end{align}
with $g_A+g_B>0$. Every other primitive is asymptotically stationary: $a\left(\cdot,t\right)\to a^\infty$, $C^N\left(\cdot,t\right)\to C^{N\infty}$, $C^D\left(\cdot,t\right)\to C^{D\infty}$, $c^c(t)\to c^{c\infty}$ and $c^T\left(\cdot,t\right)\to c^{T\infty}$, uniformly on compact sets, each limit inheriting the properties assumed in Section~\ref{sec:sp:setup:primitives}, with the yield ceiling in either case (H) or (S); the handling share $\mu$ is a constant.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:tech} isolates the two engines of progress that matter for the material question, making output from less input altogether ($g_A$) and making output from less \emph{physical} input in particular ($g_B$), while holding the recycling technology asymptotically fixed, which keeps the circularity margins from becoming a third engine. Two features of~\eqref{eq:sp:lr:trendF} should be marked at once, because much of what follows turns on them. First, material-augmenting progress multiplies the \emph{effective} input $R^e=R-\bar R$, never the floor. Technology can economise without bound on the material used above $\bar R$; it cannot economise on $\bar R$ itself, which is a statement about matter, not about knowledge. Second, the model has no endogenous growth engine by construction: the production technology is concave in capital, the recycling technology is asymptotically fixed by Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:tech}, and Appendix~\ref{sec:app:stat} adds the hypothesis under which accumulation alone cannot sustain growth. Where a result of this section says that a state \emph{requires growth}, that is its robust content; ``requires trending technology'' is the form the statement takes in this model, and Appendix~\ref{sec:app:stat} shows what the same economy does when the trend is absent.

\begin{assumption}[well-posedness and regularity]\label{ass:lr:wellposed}
Let $g$ denote the asymptotic growth rate of consumption in the cell under study, $\nu$ the asymptotic decay rate of the material flows where they decay, $\eta$ the elasticity of marginal utility, and $1+r\equiv\left(1+\rho\right)e^{\eta g}$ the asymptotic gross discount rate implied by the Euler equation~\eqref{eq:sp:sum:Cgrowth}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] \emph{Well-posedness.} $\ln\left(1+\rho\right)>\left(1-\eta\right)g$, so that lifetime utility converges along a path on which consumption grows at rate $g$. The condition is vacuous for $\eta\ge1$.
\item[(ii)] \emph{Regularity of the balanced price blocks.} $e^{g}<1+r$, which is (i) restated on a balanced path, and, on a path along which the material flows decay at rate $\nu>0$, $e^{g+\nu}<1+r$, so that a reserve rent growing with the material value is a convergent sum, together with its two consequences $\left(1-\delta\right)e^{g+\nu}<1+r$ and $\left(1-\mu\right)e^{g+\nu}<1+r$, under which the prices of embodied material and of the stockpile are.
\item[(iii)] \emph{Ranking of collapse paths.} $\left(1-\delta\right)^{1-\eta}<1+\rho$, under which a path on which production has ceased has a finite value and an interior aftermath.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

Condition (i) is what makes the objective finite; (ii) is what makes every multiplier of the balanced price blocks of Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs} finite and of the right sign; (iii) is what the shutdown construction of Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs:collapse} needs, and it is not innocuous: at $\delta=0.05$ it requires $\rho>2.6\%$ per period when $\eta=1.5$ and $\rho>5.3\%$ when $\eta=2$. Nothing in Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:wellposed} concerns the costates directly. Each costate recursion of Section~\ref{sec:sp:opt:foc} has the form $\left(1+r_{t+1}\right)m_t=\text{dividend}_{t+1}+s_{t+1}m_{t+1}$ with $s\le1$ the surviving fraction of the stock, and its general solution is the forward sum of dividends plus a bubble growing at the factor $\left(1+r\right)/s\ge1+r$, which exceeds $e^{g}$ and $e^{g+\nu}$ under (ii). Every price is tied by a first-order condition to a quantity that converges after detrending, so under Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:convergence} the bubble is zero: the forward representations of the costates reported in Section~\ref{sec:sp:opt:foc} follow from the first-order conditions and the convergence assumption, and are not assumed separately.

\begin{assumption}[convergence]\label{ass:lr:convergence}
Optimal paths settle down: every variable converges after detrending. Goods-block variables ($C$, $K$, $Y$, cost flows, prices in final goods per tonne) grow at constant asymptotic rates, and material-block variables (tonnage flows and the stocks $S$, $\bar X_{\max}-X$, $M^K$, $\mathcal W$) decay at constant asymptotic rates or converge.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[regeneration floor]\label{ass:lr:theta}
Decay is bounded away from zero, $\theta\left(P\right)\ge\theta_{\min}>0$ for all $P$, and valuation never tips: $\rho+\theta\left(P\right)+\theta'\left(P\right)P>0$ on the relevant range of $P$.
\end{assumption}

Three comments on what has been assumed away. First, Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:convergence} excludes limit cycles and more exotic attractors outright; the pollution feedback $\theta'<0$ is exactly the kind of mechanism that can generate them, and we make no claim that they cannot occur in this model, only that we do not study them. It does \emph{not} exclude multiplicity or history dependence: when several long-run states are consistent with the conditions below, which one the economy reaches may depend on initial stocks, and we condition on the limit attained. Second, Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:theta} makes pollution fully reversible: once emissions cease, the stock regenerates completely. Permanent pollution legacies, $\theta\to0$ at finite $P$, are a genuine possibility we set aside; allowing them would add a hysteresis dimension to every entry of the classification below. Third, on ranking collapse paths: two cells below have $C\to0$, and if $u$ is unbounded below ($u\left(0\right)=-\infty$, as under CRRA with $\eta\ge1$) the criterion can rank paths in such a cell only if consumption stays positive and its discounted utility converges. Condition (iii) of Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:wellposed} is exactly that: Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs:collapse} shows that consumption after a shutdown declines geometrically without reaching zero, and that collapse paths have a finite value under (iii). Where (iii) fails the ranking within a collapse cell is undefined, which is a genuine restriction the quantitative model must respect.

\subsection{The material budget and four accounting facts}\label{sec:sp:longrun:budget}

The results of this subsection require no optimality and almost no limit-taking: they are accounting, and they hold on every feasible path, with or without the technology trend. Define the \emph{material budget}
\begin{align}
\mathcal B &\equiv \left(1+\Omega^{N,S}\right)\left(S_0+\bar X_{\max}-X_0\right)+\Omega^{D,S}\left(\bar X_{\max}-X_0\right)+M^K_0+\mathcal W_0,
\label{eq:sp:lr:budget}
\end{align}
in tonnes: everything that can ever be inside the economy's circulation or enter its waste stream from outside, the extractable material and the overburden it brings up, the mass displaced by all the exploration the ceiling permits, and the material already held in the two anthropogenic stocks at date zero.

\begin{lemma}[stock bound and throughput cap]\label{lem:lr:stocks}
On every feasible path and in every period,
\begin{align}
M^K_t+\mathcal W_t \;\le\; \mathcal B,
\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad
R_t \;\le\; N_t+\bar a\,\mu\,\mathcal B .
\label{eq:sp:lr:stockbound}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

The collected ledger~\eqref{eq:sp:setup:ledger-collected} reads $\Delta\left(M^K+\mathcal W\right)_{t+1}=N_t+\mathcal N_t-\left(1-a_t\varpi_t\right)H_t\le N_t+\mathcal N_t$; summing and using the cumulative bounds on $N$ and $D$ from~\eqref{eq:sp:sum:cumulative-N} and $X_{t+1}-X_t=D_t$ gives the first inequality. The second is the throughput cap~\eqref{eq:sp:setup:throughput-cap} with the stock replaced by its bound. \emph{Circular supply is bounded in every period, under both ceiling cases}: however close $a\varpi$ comes to one, recycled material cannot exceed $\bar a\mu$ tonnes per tonne of retained anthropogenic stock, and the stock can never exceed the budget.

\begin{lemma}[cumulated leakage]\label{lem:lr:leakage}
On every feasible path and for every horizon $T$,
\begin{align}
\sum_{t=0}^{T} \left(1-a_t\varpi_t\right)H_t \;\le\; \mathcal B .
\label{eq:sp:lr:leakage}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

This is the same summation read on the other side: cumulated leakage equals cumulated inflows plus the net rundown of the two stocks, both bounded by the budget. Lemma~\ref{lem:lr:leakage} holds under both ceilings and is the general form from which everything ceiling-specific follows.

\begin{lemma}[finite cumulative throughput under the hard ceiling]\label{lem:lr:throughput}
Under the hard ceiling (H), on every feasible path,
\begin{align}
\sum_{t=0}^\infty H_t \;\le\; \frac{\mathcal B}{1-\bar a},
\qquad
\sum_{t=0}^\infty R_t \;\le\; \left(S_0+\bar X_{\max}-X_0\right)+\frac{\bar a\,\mathcal B}{1-\bar a}
\;\equiv\;\bar{\mathcal R},
\qquad
\sum_{t=0}^\infty W_t \;\le\; \frac{\mathcal B}{1-\bar a}+\mathcal B .
\label{eq:sp:lr:throughput}
\end{align}
If in addition $\bar R>0$, the material era has bounded duration: the number of periods in which production runs is at most
\begin{align}
\#\left\{t:R_t\ge\bar R\right\} \;\le\; \bar{\mathcal R}/\bar R .
\label{eq:sp:lr:duration}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

The first bound follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:lr:leakage} and $1-a\varpi\ge1-\bar a>0$; the second from $R=N+a\varpi H$; the third from $W=\Delta\mathcal W+H$ and the stock bound; the last from $\sum_t R_t\ge\bar R\cdot\#\left\{R_t\ge\bar R\right\}$. This is the circularity multiplier in cumulated form: recycling lets each tonne of the budget serve production at most $\left(1-\bar a\right)^{-1}$ times, and no feasible policy, however capital-intensive its recycling and however patient its planner, can do better. Under the hard ceiling, physical throughput, waste and pollution are therefore \emph{transitional} phenomena on every feasible path: the flows are summable, under Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:convergence} they converge to zero, and with the regeneration floor $P\to0$.

The soft ceiling is another matter, and the next result says exactly what escaping Lemma~\ref{lem:lr:throughput} costs.

\begin{proposition}[growth is a precondition for perpetual circularity]\label{prop:lr:growth}
Suppose a feasible path holds $R_t\ge\bar R>0$ for all $t\ge T_0$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the yield ceiling is soft ($\bar a=1$) and $\sum^\infty\left(1-a_t\varpi_t\right)<\infty$, so $a\varpi\to1$; recycling capital per treated tonne diverges, $x\to\infty$, and so does recycling capital itself, $K^R\to\infty$;
\item the retained stock covers the floor forever,
\begin{align}
\mathcal W_t \;\ge\; \frac{\bar R-N_t}{\bar a\,\mu}
\qquad\text{with }N_t\to0,
\label{eq:sp:lr:survival}
\end{align}
so that $\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal W_t\ge\bar R/\left(\bar a\,\mu\right)=\bar R/\mu$ under the soft ceiling: the anthropogenic reserve, turning over at share $\mu$ per period, must itself carry the floor.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The proof chains the accounting. Since $\sum_t N_t<\infty$, eventually $N_t<\bar R/2$ and recycled supply must cover the rest, $a\varpi\mu\mathcal W\ge\bar R-N$, which is~\eqref{eq:sp:lr:survival} and bounds $H=\mu\mathcal W$ away from zero. Lemma~\ref{lem:lr:leakage} with $H$ bounded below forces $\sum\left(1-a\varpi\right)<\infty$, which is impossible under a hard ceiling and, under the soft ceiling, forces $a\to1$ and hence $x=K^R/\left(\varpi H\right)\to\infty$; since $\varpi H\ge a\varpi H\ge\bar R-N$ is bounded below, $K^R=x\varpi H\to\infty$ as well. The proposition is the model's central feasibility fact: \emph{a closed material loop is not a low-tech steady state but a capital-intensive frontier.} Maintaining an unbounded capital stock requires unboundedly growing replacement output, which is why the loop needs a growing economy; Appendix~\ref{sec:app:stat} shows that a stationary economy cannot afford it, and under Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:tech} the question becomes whether the tail of the yield function makes the diverging recycling intensity worth paying for. That is a question about values, not feasibility, and Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs:circular} settles it.

\subsection{The three long-run states}\label{sec:sp:longrun:class}

Classify a long-run configuration by what the material block does. By the cumulative bound~\eqref{eq:sp:sum:cumulative-N}, virgin extraction cannot stay bounded away from zero, so in the long run the material block is either \emph{dead} (production has ceased), \emph{vanishing} (material flows strictly positive but converging to zero) or a \emph{perpetual loop} ($N\to0$ with $R$ bounded away from zero, sustained by recycling alone). With a floor the vanishing block is not available: flows below $\bar R$ mean production has ceased, so with $\bar R>0$ the material era ends discretely or not at all. Three states cover the possibilities, and we define them in turn.

\begin{definition}[collapse, state A]\label{def:lr:collapse}
Let $\mathcal O\equiv\left\{t:R_t\ge\bar R\right\}$ be the set of periods in which production runs. A path is in state A if $\mathcal O$ is finite: production ceases after a finite total number of operating periods, output is zero thereafter, and consumption converges to zero along the aftermath in which the economy consumes its remaining capital stock.
\end{definition}

The definition bounds the number of operating periods, not their arrangement. $\mathcal O$ may be a finite union of intervals rather than a single one, because Appendix~\ref{sec:app:suff:regimes} records that a shutdown need not be absorbing: an idle economy digests its capital stock into the waste stock and may be able to restart. What state A rules out is an infinite total operating time. After the last operating period $Y=0$, and the goods constraint~\eqref{eq:sp:setup:resource-constraint} leaves consumption to be financed from the capital stock alone, $C_t\le\left(1-\delta\right)K_t-K_{t+1}$, so $K\to0$ and $C\to0$. Two features of the aftermath belong to the state rather than to any construction. First, \emph{trending technology does not rescue the economy}: $B_t$ multiplies $R-\bar R$ and cannot economise on the floor, so growth changes the level of consumption extracted during the material era, possibly enormously, and the timing of the shutdown, but not the verdict. Second, the economy leaves a pollution echo. After the shutdown there is no output to pay for treatment, so the stockpile drains to the environment untreated, $\Xi_t=\mu\mathcal W_t$, while the capital being consumed keeps releasing its embodied material into it, $W_t=\delta M^K_t$: the stock runs down geometrically at factor $1-\mu$ on an inflow that runs down at factor $1-\delta$. At the buffer corner $\mu=1$ the stockpile is emptied in one period and the legacy stream is the $\delta M^K$ inflow alone. Collapse is followed by a \emph{legacy emission stream}, landfills outliving the economy that filled them, after which $P\to0$ by the regeneration floor.

\begin{definition}[balanced dematerialization path, state B]\label{def:lr:bdp}
A path is a balanced dematerialization path (BDP, state B) if, asymptotically, the goods block grows at a constant rate $g$, with $C,K,Y$ at rate $g$ and $\ln\left(1+r\right)\to\ln\left(1+\rho\right)+\eta g$, while the material block decays exponentially: $R,N,W,H,T,M^K$ at rate $\nu>0$, with $S$ and $\bar X_{\max}-X$ converging to zero, and the recycling margins converging, $a\to\bar a$ and $\varpi$ into its upper branch.
\end{definition}

The sign of $g$ is an outcome of rate matching, not part of the definition: the material budget constrains cumulated \emph{physical} input, and sustained progress converts a shrinking physical flow into non-shrinking production, either because effective materials $B_tR$ hold up as $R$ decays ($g_B>0$) or because TFP growth outpaces the drag from a declining material input ($g_A>0$), but whether the result is growth, a sustained level or managed decline depends on how fast extraction costs rise as the reserve is squeezed. A state-B path exists only without a floor: with $\bar R>0$, $R$ cannot decay through $\bar R$ while production continues, and Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs:bdp} records what the definition requires of the budget and of the stockpile.

\begin{definition}[perpetual circular growth, state C]\label{def:lr:circular}
A path is in state C if, asymptotically, the resource side dies, $N,D\to0$ with $\sum_tN_t<\infty$; material input converges to a strictly positive level met entirely by recycled material, $R\to R_\infty>\bar R$ with $R^R\to R_\infty$; the loop closes, $a\varpi\to1$ with $x\to\infty$; the two anthropogenic stocks converge; and the goods block grows at a constant rate $g>0$.
\end{definition}

The rest of the configuration follows. The stockpile converges to the level that turns over the loop, $\mathcal W_\infty=R_\infty/\mu$, which satisfies the survival bound~\eqref{eq:sp:lr:survival}; leakage $\left(1-a\varpi\right)\mu\mathcal W$ is summable, so $\Xi\to0$ and $P\to0$; the material intensity $\Omega\propto R/Y$ decays at rate $g$; and the goods block grows, in the workhorse's Cobb--Douglas case at
\begin{align}
g &= \frac{g_A+\gamma\,g_B}{1-\beta_K},
\label{eq:sp:lr:c2-growth}
\end{align}
with $\beta_K$ and $\gamma$ the output elasticities of capital and materials, the balanced rate of an economy whose physical material input is asymptotically \emph{constant} while its effective input $B_tR^e_\infty$ grows at $g_B$. Consumption grows without bound on a fixed diet of matter: with a floor, the circular economy is not an efficiency improvement but the entire survival condition, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:lr:growth} it is affordable only because the economy grows.

\emph{The circulating flow obeys Little's law, and survival is a stock condition.} Summing the collected ledger over all periods defines the \emph{retained material endowment} $\mathcal M_\infty\equiv\lim_t\left(M^K_t+\mathcal W_t\right)\le\mathcal B$, the budget less what was never extracted and what leaked on the way. On a state-C path the two anthropogenic stocks are stationary, so each is its flow times its residence time, and
\begin{align}
R_\infty &= \frac{\mathcal M_\infty}{\mathcal T},
&
\mathcal T &\equiv \frac{1}{\mu}+\frac{\sigma_\infty}{\delta},
&&\text{so survival requires}
&
\mathcal M_\infty &> \bar R\,\mathcal T,
\label{eq:sp:lr:little}
\end{align}
with $\mathcal T$ the mean number of periods a tonne spends standing still per pass, waiting in the stockpile and locked in durables at the storage share $\sigma_\infty$. Since $\mathcal M_\infty\le\mathcal B$ and $\mathcal T\ge1/\mu$, a necessary condition in primitives alone is $\mu\mathcal B>\bar R$: the entire material budget, turning over at the handling share, must clear the floor. The direction of the durability term is worth marking, because it is counter-intuitive: for a given retained endowment, a \emph{more} durable economy supports a \emph{smaller} sustainable throughput, since a larger share of its matter is not moving. Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs:circular} derives~\eqref{eq:sp:lr:little} from the two stock transitions alone.

\subsection{Taxonomy}\label{sec:sp:longrun:taxonomy}

The classification is the table below. ``Ceiling'' distinguishes the hard ceiling (H) from the soft ceiling (S) of Section~\ref{sec:sp:setup:primitives}. Two conditions split the soft rows. The \emph{closure criterion} of Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs:circular} asks whether the yield tail lets the loop close at a cost the economy can pay: the leakage a closed loop leaves behind must be cumulatively finite, as Lemma~\ref{lem:lr:leakage} demands, and the criterion is met by the exponential and power tails and fails for tails whose elasticity vanishes, of which the logarithmic family is the leading example. The \emph{survival condition} $\mathcal M_\infty>\bar R\,\mathcal T$ of~\eqref{eq:sp:lr:little} asks whether the matter the economy retains clears the floor at its own turnover rate. Pollution converges to zero in every row; in the collapse rows it does so after the legacy emission stream of the draining stockpile.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
$\bar R$ & Ceiling & State & Long-run $C$ \\
\midrule
$0$ & hard & B: balanced dematerialization & grows at $g$ by rate matching (Section~\ref{sec:sp:longrun:sketches}) \\
$0$ & soft & C if the loop closes, else B & grows at $g>0$; else as in B \\
\midrule
$>0$ & hard & A: collapse \emph{despite growth} & $C\to0$ \\
$>0$ & soft & C if the loop closes and $\mathcal M_\infty>\bar R\mathcal T$, else A & grows at $g>0$; else $C\to0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Four readings organise the table.

\emph{First, the floor turns survival into a stock condition.} Without a floor every cell has a surviving state: the material block shrinks and progress makes the shrinking flow go further, so the only question is how fast the economy grows. With a floor the only surviving state is C, and whether it is reached is a condition on a stock: the retained endowment must clear the floor at the economy's own turnover rate. Whether materials are essential to the dematerialized technology, the question that organises the stationary benchmark of Appendix~\ref{sec:app:stat}, decides nothing here: with a floor, level essentiality holds by construction, and marginal essentiality is pre-empted by the discrete shutdown of Section~\ref{sec:sp:opt:foc}, which stops material use at a strictly positive flow. This is a large simplification, and it is the floor doing it.

\emph{Second, growth is not a free pass.} Trending technology rescues the floorless economy unconditionally, but it rescues the floor economy only through the closed loop: under a hard ceiling the material era has bounded duration whatever $g_A$ and $g_B$ are, because progress augments effective materials and cannot touch the floor. The cell (H)$\times\bar R>0$, a technologically brilliant economy collapsing on schedule, is the taxonomy's starkest entry.

\emph{Third, the bottom-right cell is the paper's sharpest claim.} With a floor, survival and perpetual circularity are the same thing, available only under the soft ceiling, affordable only because the economy grows (Proposition~\ref{prop:lr:growth}), and reachable only if the economy retains enough matter to clear the floor at its own turnover rate, $\mathcal M_\infty>\bar R\mathcal T$ by~\eqref{eq:sp:lr:little}. In every other row the circular economy is a transition technology: it determines how much value the budget $\mathcal B$ yields and how much pollution damage is suffered en route, which is what \quantnote{} measures, while in this one cell it is the difference between growth forever and collapse. The scarcity reading of circularity survives throughout: full treatment and ceiling-level yields emerge asymptotically wherever materials stay scarce, and precisely because they stay scarce.

\emph{Fourth, what the classification is not.} Two columns of the table are primitives that no policy moves, the floor and the ceiling, and the trend is a statement about technology. Nothing in the table is a choice. What \emph{is} a choice is which side of the survival inequality~\eqref{eq:sp:lr:little} an economy in the bottom-right cell ends up on, since $\mathcal M_\infty$ is the endogenous outcome of every extraction and leakage decision made along the way. The taxonomy classifies the destinations; the transition decides which one is reached, and that is a quantitative question.

\subsection{Sketches of the arguments}\label{sec:sp:longrun:sketches}

One paragraph per state says where each entry of the table comes from; Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs} carries the arguments, one subsection per state, and marks in each which results hold for general primitives and which are constructed for the workhorse.

\emph{Collapse (A).} Under the hard ceiling the argument is Lemma~\ref{lem:lr:throughput}: with $\bar R>0$ production can run at or above the floor for at most $\bar{\mathcal R}/\bar R$ periods in total, whatever the trend, so $\mathcal O$ is finite. Under the soft ceiling the argument runs through the survival condition. If $\mathcal O$ were infinite, Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:convergence} would make it cofinite, Proposition~\ref{prop:lr:growth} would close the loop and make the stockpile carry the floor, and Little's law would give $R_\infty=\mathcal M_\infty/\mathcal T\ge\bar R$; so a path whose retained endowment fails the survival condition, or whose yield tail fails the closure criterion, has a finite $\mathcal O$. Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs:collapse} proves both and, for the workhorse, makes the shutdown explicit: during the era the material value obeys Hotelling's rule applied to the recycling-multiplied budget, so circularity lowers the level of the price path without touching its slope, which is the precise sense in which it is a transition technology here; the shutdown date is a discrete comparison; and after it the economy is a cake-eating problem in its own depreciating capital stock, with consumption declining geometrically at factor $\left[\left(1-\delta\right)/\left(1+\rho\right)\right]^{1/\eta}$, which is what makes the value of stopping computable and hence the shutdown date itself.

\emph{Perpetual circular growth (C).} The state is consistent with the accounting exactly when the leakage it leaves behind is cumulatively finite (Lemma~\ref{lem:lr:leakage}), and its level is fixed by Little's law~\eqref{eq:sp:lr:little}. Whether the loop closes is a statement about the tail of the yield function. Along the path the recycling-capital margin~\eqref{eq:sp:sum:KR-interior} pins the recycling intensity by $a'\left(x_t\right)=\tilde F_{K,t}/\mathcal G^K_t$, where $\tilde F_K\equiv F_K\left(1-\zeta\Omega^Y\right)$ is the material-adjusted marginal product of capital; the ratio converges to zero because the value of recovered material grows with the economy while the marginal product of capital does not, so the margin is always satisfiable and $x_t\to\infty$ automatically. What is not automatic is that the leakage rate $1-a\left(x_t\right)$ is summable, and Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs:circular} shows that it equals the recycling capital cost per treated tonne, relative to the value of recovered material, deflated by the elasticity of the yield tail. Summability of that product is the closure criterion. For the workhorse the exponential tail closes the loop with $x_t$ rising linearly in time and a recycling sector whose capital share vanishes, the power tail closes it for every exponent, and logarithmic tails fail; the appendix also solves the state-C price block in closed form and records its signs, $p^W<0$ and $\zeta<0$: on a closed loop waste is the last mine, and at the margin durability competes with it.

\emph{Balanced dematerialization (B).} Without a floor, exponentially decaying flows are summable, so every cumulative bound of Section~\ref{sec:sp:longrun:budget} binds only in the limit and $\Xi\to0$ with $P\to0$; the stockpile rides along at the decay rate of the flows when $\mu>1-e^{-\nu}$, and otherwise its drainage at factor $1-\mu$ is the slowest material reservoir and caps the decay of the whole block. The rates $\left(g,\nu\right)$ are determined jointly by the production side and the extraction margin, and they must be solved jointly: in the workhorse's Cobb--Douglas case the two rows are $\left(1-\beta_K\right)g=g_A+\gamma\left(g_B-\nu\right)$ and $\left(\mu_N-1\right)\nu=g$, giving
\begin{align}
g &= \frac{g_A+\gamma\left(g_B-\nu\right)}{1-\beta_K}
\;=\;\frac{\left(\mu_N-1\right)\left(g_A+\gamma g_B\right)}{\left(\mu_N-1\right)\left(1-\beta_K\right)+\gamma},
&
\nu &= \frac{g_A+\gamma g_B}{\left(\mu_N-1\right)\left(1-\beta_K\right)+\gamma} ,
\label{eq:sp:lr:bdp-growth}
\end{align}
where the stock-effect elasticity of extraction costs $\mu_N$ selects the outcome: $\mu_N>1$ gives sustained growth, $\mu_N=1$ a sustained consumption \emph{level}, with material-augmenting progress exactly offsetting physical decay, and $\mu_N<1$ managed decline, the exponential family failing altogether once $\gamma\le\left(1-\mu_N\right)\left(1-\beta_K\right)$, where extraction ends in finite time and the path continues on recycled material alone. Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs:bdp} carries the feasibility lemma, the full rate table and the derivation.

\emph{Selection.} Where state C is feasible and the alternative is collapse, it is selected whenever $u$ ranks a growing stream above one converging to zero, which under condition (iii) of Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:wellposed} is a comparison of finite values; this is the honest sense in which state C is the survival route. Where $\bar R=0$ and the alternative is state B, the two workhorse rates share a numerator and
\begin{align}
g_{\mathrm{B}} &= \frac{\left(\mu_N-1\right)\left(1-\beta_K\right)}{\left(\mu_N-1\right)\left(1-\beta_K\right)+\gamma}\;g_{\mathrm{C}}\;<\;g_{\mathrm{C}} .
\label{eq:sp:lr:bdp-vs-c}
\end{align}
\emph{Balanced dematerialization always grows strictly more slowly than the closed loop}, and the entire gap is the physical decay $\nu$ that the squeeze path accepts and the loop does not. Since closing the loop costs an asymptotically vanishing share of the capital stock, the planner closes it whenever the yield tail allows: state C dominates state B wherever both are feasible, and the floorless soft-ceiling cell is a C cell, not a comparison (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:proofs:circular}). What the floor changes is not the choice but the stakes: without it, choosing B costs growth; with it, B is not available at all.

\smalltitle{Configurations to check, not to classify.} Three path properties cut across the table. \emph{Skiba points:} the pollution non-convexity ($\theta'<0$) can strand the economy at an inferior long-run state; we condition on the limit attained. \emph{Tipping:} $r+\theta+\theta'P\le0$ makes $p^P$ diverge; excluded by Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:theta}, which is not innocuous. \emph{Utility degeneracy:} the collapse rows cannot be ranked internally when $u\left(0\right)=-\infty$ unless condition (iii) of Assumption~\ref{ass:lr:wellposed} holds; see the scope remark in Section~\ref{sec:sp:longrun:scope}.

\smalltitle{Open items.} In decreasing order of importance. (i)~Sufficiency, which Appendix~\ref{sec:app:suff} takes as far as it goes. Given the operating set and the material-intensity path, the necessary conditions above are sufficient under four checkable conditions on primitives, and transversality reduces to a single condition on capital because mass conservation bounds the other five states. What is not established is optimality against deviations that change the intensity path, optimality across operating sets where a shutdown might not be permanent, and sufficiency where extraction has a finite choke value, which is exactly the configuration in which stranding, and hence multiplicity, is possible. (ii)~The transition into state C, in particular how much of the budget an optimal path retains, which by~\eqref{eq:sp:lr:little} fixes the level of the entire future and which only the quantitative model can answer. (iii)~The regularity restrictions the state-C price block imposes on calibrations (Appendix~\ref{sec:app:workhorse:cbgp}), which are inequalities on wedges rather than conditions on primitives. (iv)~The rate algebra of states B and C away from Cobb--Douglas. (v)~The irreversible-pollution variant, which adds a hysteresis dimension to every row.
